# Keep the detector, fix the quorum

The prior art mostly backs TRON's per-peer detectors. It pushes back hard on how their outputs are fused. The 4-of-6 missed-beacon window is the textbook binary-integration rule for a six-sample window. Against the simulator's fault patterns it detects about as fast as a false-alarm-matched Page CUSUM. It also catches the intermittent severity-2 and severity-3 faults, which a phi-accrual detector cannot, so the voter side (§2.1) should stay largely as it is. The weaknesses sit in §3.3. First, `x_min` is recomputed from `n_live`, so silence lowers the bar. Byzantine quorum theory forbids this: thresholds are fixed against the configured membership. Second, `y` is labelled Brooks–Iyengar but meets none of that algorithm's preconditions. Third, the false-ELEVATED estimates assume independent per-link loss, which correlated-voter theory says is the optimistic case. The biggest missed opportunity is also in fusion. The design fuses binary votes, but a communication fault silences the target on the same ticks for every peer. Fusing a per-tick "who did I hear" bitmap would detect at fault tick 1 or 2 instead of about fault tick 5 plus a round. That goes straight at risk R3 for 2 extra bytes per beacon. For mobility, swarm-robotics and VANET work agree that votes derived from a target's own position reports cannot catch a consistent liar (R1). Cheap plausibility checks help, and physical-layer evidence such as RSSI is the only known fix. The prioritized changes are at the end.

A note on evidence. Many numbers below are exact binomial or Markov-chain calculations that the research team ran under the design's own model (i.i.d. loss *q* per link per tick, 9 observers per target). They are marked "computed". They reproduce the design's own figures, for example 8.6·10⁻⁵ and 1.7·10⁻² for a false 4-of-6 vote at *q* = 0.05 and 0.20. They are analysis, not published results.

## The 4-of-6 window is the right detector, and phi-accrual would miss TRON's faults

Chandra–Toueg's failure-detector classes are the wrong yardstick for this detector. They only describe what happens "eventually". Chen, Toueg and Aguilera point out that a detector that suspects a crash an hour late still solves asynchronous consensus but is useless to an application with deadlines ([Chen et al., DSN 2000](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2000/06/dsn00_fdqos.pdf)). Their probabilistic QoS framework does fit. Under message loss *p_L* > 0, **mistakes recur at a positive rate forever**, and the designer picks the mean mistake-recurrence time rather than eliminating mistakes ([Chen et al. 2000, Prop. 3 and Thm. 4](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2000/06/dsn00_fdqos.pdf)). In that frame, the 4-of-6 rule has strong completeness with a bounded detection time: a silent target is suspected by every observer within 4 counted ticks of a full window, whatever *q* is. It has no eventual accuracy under Bernoulli loss. Its accuracy is the binomial tail. Computed exactly as a 64-state sliding-window chain, the mean time between false FAULT votes per observer–target pair is **19,157 ticks at q = 0.05, 1,443 at q = 0.10 and only 129 at q = 0.20**. At *q* = 0.2 that means about 1.5 false FAULT votes are standing somewhere among the 90 pairs at any tick (computed). Folding late beacons into misses matches the semantics of Chen's NFD-S detector, where a heartbeat that arrives after its freshness point does not restore trust.

The choice of M = 4 is not arbitrary. The radar rule of thumb for M-of-N binary integration, **M = 0.955·N^0.8**, gives 4.00 for N = 6, and M "is not a sensitive selection" ([MathWorks binaryintloss](https://www.mathworks.com/help/radar/ref/binaryintloss.html)). Optimal sequential tests do not beat it by much. CUSUM is exactly optimal under Lorden's minimax criterion ([Moustakides 1986](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176350164); [Xie et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04186)), and a Bernoulli CUSUM is the standard chart for item-by-item proportions ([Reynolds & Stoumbos 1999](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00224065.1999.11979900)). With its false-alarm rate matched to 4-of-6, it gives a mean alarm tick of 4.86 / 4.67 / 3.89 on severity-2 faults at *q* = 0.05 / 0.10 / 0.20. The window gives 4.82 / 4.59 / 4.03 (computed). CUSUM wins clearly only at *q* = 0.2 with severity 3 (2.70 against 3.71). The underlying limit is informational. Delay scales as log(ARL₀)/KL, and one beacon carries at most about 1.4 nats. **No single-observer rule can detect much faster than 5 ticks at these false-alarm levels, so R3 is fundamental on the per-peer side.** The transient-change literature adds that when faults have a finite duration and "detect before it ends" is the goal, a finite-moving-average window is the optimal form of window-limited CUSUM ([Guépié, Fillatre & Nikiforov 2012](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07474946.2012.719443)). The window rule is the correct shape for this problem, not a crude stand-in.

Phi-accrual would be a regression. Hayashibara's φ = −log10(P_later(time since last heartbeat)) ([Hayashibara et al., SRDS 2004](https://classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2026/spring/23380-1/papers/hayashibara_phi.pdf)) is deployed with **thresholds around 8** and windows of 200–1000 samples ([Akka docs](https://doc.akka.io/libraries/akka-core/current/typed/failure-detector.html); [Cassandra FailureDetector.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apache/cassandra/trunk/src/java/org/apache/cassandra/gms/FailureDetector.java)). On a slotted bus with i.i.d. loss, φ after *m* silent ticks is just −m·log10(q). Every received beacon resets it. A target that emits every 3rd tick therefore never pushes φ above about 1.4 at *q* = 0.2 (computed), and a pure φ detector would never flag TRON's intermittent communication faults. The windowed miss count measures "reduced delivery rate", and φ measures "stopped". The design picked the right statistic.

Two refinements come from this literature. The first concerns the self-deaf rule. Lifeguard is its closest published analogue, and its data confirm the premise: **false positives are "dominated by those occurring at slow processing members"**. Lifeguard's graded Local Health Multiplier and its suspicion-count timeout cut total false positives to **1.53 % of SWIM's**, at a cost of about 0.1 s in median detection latency ([Dadgar, Phillips & Currey, Lifeguard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.00788)). The TRON rule is binary. It fires only on ticks where the observer heard nobody, which a healthy observer hits with probability q⁹ ≈ 5·10⁻⁷ at *q* = 0.2 (computed). An observer with a degraded but not dead receiver hears someone most ticks, so it would vote FAULT on several peers at once without being filtered. The second refinement concerns CLEAR, which needs a spotless window. At *q* = 0.2 only **26 % of observer-ticks can vote CLEAR, and 72 % abstain**. Allowing CLEAR at ≤ 1 miss raises that to 65.5 % (computed). That matters more than it looks, because CLEAR votes are what the `|F| > |C|` guard relies on, as the next section shows.

## Letting silence lower the bar breaks quorum theory

The fusion rule in §3.3 borrows vocabulary from Brooks–Iyengar and Byzantine quorums, but it drops the invariants that make those results hold. Byzantine quorum constructions fix a fault budget *f* **in advance over the whole membership**. They then require *f*+1 matching witnesses (at least one correct) or 2*f*+1 (correct witnesses outnumber faulty ones). Masking quorums need intersections of at least 2*b*+1 and exist only when 4*b* < *n* ([Malkhi, Reiter & Wool](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/9908011)). The design's `x_min = max(2, floor((n_live−1)/3)+1)` is the *f*+1 witness rule, but it computes *f* from whoever was heard. With 9 peers configured, *f* = 2 and three agreeing domains are needed. If three peers go dark, `n_live` = 6, *f* drops to 1 and two voters suffice. Faulty nodes going silent is itself inside the Byzantine budget. Letting that silence lower the threshold is the anti-pattern quorum sizing exists to prevent.

The majority guard makes this worse rather than better. Excluding abstainers from the denominator is standard parliamentary practice. It is also epistemically sound when abstainers are the less-informed voters: the swing voter's curse shows that uninformed voters with common interests should abstain, and outcomes still match full information ([Feddersen & Pesendorfer 1996](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Swing-Voter's-Curse-Feddersen-Pesendorfer/ffe4b7b9e1f5f5654a1538f58a1b17762c4b69b9)). ABSTAIN in TRON is mechanical (an incomplete window), so the principle holds. The known pitfall, familiar from quorum rules in referendums, is that **lowering turnout lowers the bar** ([Aguiar-Conraria & Magalhães 2010](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S017626801000011X)). In this design, abstention and darkness lower `|C|` and `x_min` at the same time. Take the design's own two-liar test (§7.3) with three honest peers dark instead of zero. Two liars vote FAULT, `x_min` = 2, and the four live honest peers must produce at least two CLEARs to block. At *q* = 0.2, where each honest peer says CLEAR with probability 0.262, **two liars elevate a healthy target in 72 % of such rounds**. At *q* = 0.05 the figure is still 6 % (computed). Under i.i.d. loss, three or more of the seven honest peers go dark at the fuser in only about 0.2 % of rounds at *q* = 0.2 (computed). Under burst loss or a `vbus_set_node_dark` event it is routine. With `x_min` fixed at 3 from the configured membership, the two liars can never elevate, whatever the turnout.

The rest of §3.3 has a related problem: it assumes voters are independent. The design estimates about 4·10⁻⁴ false ELEVATED per target-round by multiplying independent per-link error rates. Correlated-voter theory is blunt about this. Positive correlation lowers majority accuracy, and at perfect correlation the jury is **exactly as accurate as one juror**, M_n(p,1) = p ([Kaniovski](https://serguei.kaniovski.wifo.ac.at/fileadmin/pdf/condorcet.pdf)). When votes share a common cause, reliability converges to a value below 1 however many voters there are ([Dietrich & Spiekermann](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/101162/1/Spiekermann_Jury_theorems.pdf)). A real fault is a benign common cause that drives every peer to FAULT. A collision burst, a TX-timing artifact or a round-boundary effect is a misleading one, and adding voters does not remove it. Real loss is bursty: Hayashibara's one-week WAN trace saw 814 loss bursts, the longest 1093 heartbeats ([Hayashibara 2004 §5](https://classes.cs.uchicago.edu/archive/2026/spring/23380-1/papers/hayashibara_phi.pdf)). Kaniovski and Zaigraev show that under positive correlation, quotas above a "correlation-robust" level *gain* reliability as correlation rises and bare majority sits below that level ([Kaniovski & Zaigraev 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11238-009-9170-2)). That argues for a supermajority or a fixed 2*f*+1 = 5 witnesses, not `|F| > |C|`. The design says 7–9 peers see a real fault, so either costs little detection.

The `y` statistic cannot bear the name Brooks–Iyengar. Every guarantee in the Marzullo, Brooks–Iyengar and approximate-agreement family rests on two assumptions: the true value lies in **every correct interval**, and τ is fixed over **all N inputs** ([Rushby 2002](https://www.csl.sri.com/~rushby/papers/sensors.pdf); [Ao et al. 2016](http://users.cis.fiu.edu/~iyengar/publication/On-Precision-Bound-of-Distributed-Fault-Tolerant-Sensor-Fusion-Algorithms-2015.pdf)). TRON fuses only the FAULT subset. It uses the maximum-overlap region, which amounts to a τ chosen from the data. Its peers' Wilson intervals estimate *different* per-link quantities, and Wilson's minimum coverage at n ≤ 6 is only 75–79 % (computed). So `y` is a robust central value among voters who already said FAULT. It is not a fault-masked estimate, and it throws away the CLEAR voters' low-severity evidence. Marzullo-style fusion is also discontinuous, the flaw Lamport criticised, so `y` will flap around `CONSENSUS_TAU_LOCAL` as one interval crosses another. Schmid and Schossmaier's Fault-Tolerant Interval fixes both problems. It takes the (*f*+1)-th largest lower bound over all live opinionated intervals, is Lipschitz, and is "optimal among all such functions" ([Schmid & Schossmaier 2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/PL00008927)). It also has a clean reading: at least one correct peer is confident severity is at least `y`. Since LOCAL has no behavioural effect, this is a correctness-of-meaning fix, not a safety fix. Wilson itself is fine. Brown, Cai and DasGupta recommend it for small n ([Brown, Cai & DasGupta 2001](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-16/issue-2/Interval-Estimation-for-a-Binomial-Proportion/10.1214/ss/1009213286.full)), and Clopper–Pearson's guaranteed coverage would cost 25–30 % more width for no behavioural gain.

## Binary votes waste the common-mode signal that fires at fault tick 1

The largest gain in the whole review comes from what the peers send, not from how each one decides. A communication fault silences the target on **the same ticks for every peer**, while healthy losses are independent per link. The per-tick count *M_t* of peers that missed the target is therefore very informative. The rule "*M_t* ≥ 8 of 9" alarms at **fault tick 1 with probability 1**. Its mean time to false alarm is about **5.3·10⁴ ticks at q = 0.2 and 3·10⁹ at q = 0.05** (computed). The current pipeline makes each peer wait for its own 4-of-6 window, then fuses votes over a 2-tick round, so it cannot alarm before about fault tick 4–5 plus the round. Decentralized change-detection theory predicts this ordering. Tartakovsky and Veeravalli prove that fusing local *decisions* is first-order asymptotically optimal, but that it performs worse than fusing quantized *observations* "unless the false alarm rate … is unreasonably small" ([Tartakovsky & Veeravalli 2008](https://vvv.ece.illinois.edu/papers/journal/tart-Veer-sqa-2008.pdf)). TRON sits squarely in the practical regime.

The implementation is cheap. Each beacon adds a 10-bit heard-from bitmap: which peers' beacons for the previous tick arrived on time. That grows the payload from 50 to 52 bytes. By the design's own AN1200.13 arithmetic, time on air goes from 138.5 ms to **143.6 ms**, and duty cycle from 0.385 % to 0.40 % (computed), still well inside the 1 % reference. The fuser at node *t* learns about tick *k* from beacons sent at *k*+1, so a verdict can form around fault tick 2. The same bitmaps give every node the full 10×10 heard-matrix. That generalises the self-deaf rule properly: a row of misses is the observer's own radio, and a column of misses is the target's fault. The graded Lifeguard idea becomes a row-sum test instead of an all-or-nothing gate.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, raw counts are more exposed to lying reporters than votes are. Bayraktar and Lai show that even one Byzantine sensor can turn a centralized CUSUM's delay from logarithmic to linear in the false-alarm interval ([Bayraktar & Lai, arXiv:1306.2086](https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.2086)). The threshold must therefore be sized for *f* = 2 liars. Requiring all 9 bits to show a miss leaves 7 honest peers that must all miss, which happens with probability q⁷ ≈ 1.3·10⁻⁵ per tick at *q* = 0.2 (computed). A liar claiming "heard" can block the fast path, though, so the existing vote path must stay as the fallback. Second, the same correlated-loss caveat applies with more force. A transmitter-side collision or fade that is not a fault looks exactly like a column of misses. The fast path needs the correlated-loss tests as much as the vote path does.

## Peers vote on the target's own story, the known blind spot of swarm and VANET detectors

The communication vote has the soundest pedigree in this literature. Christensen, O'Grady and Dorigo's firefly swarms detect failed robots by **the absence of an expected periodic signal** on the observer's own receiver ([Christensen et al. 2009](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TEVC.2009.2017516)). That is TRON's §2.1 exactly, and it is why a target cannot report its way out of a communication fault. WSN self-diagnosis in the PMC lineage adds the principle that votes are only as independent as the evidence behind them ([Preparata, Metze & Chien 1967](https://doi.org/10.1109/PGEC.1967.264748)). WSN neighbour comparison works because neighbouring sensors measure the same physical field ([Ding et al. 2005](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4165543_Localized_fault-tolerant_event_boundary_detection_in_sensor_networks)). TRON's mobility voters measure nothing about the target; they all replay its self-reported positions. By that standard the effective test graph has one tester. That is R1 and R2 in the literature's terms.

VANET misbehaviour detection faces R1 directly and has measured it. TRON's stall rule is VeReMi's **distance-moved verifier (DMV)**. VeReMi found DMV strong against an "eventual stop" sender, which is TRON's honest-but-stalled case, but **it does not detect a constant-offset liar at all**, and speed-consistency checks stay at 0.3 precision against constant offsets ([van der Heijden, Lukaseder & Kargl, VeReMi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06701)). Swarm robotics reports the same pattern. Tarapore et al.'s peer-voted detector reaches **0.84–0.89 detection with ≤ 1 % false positives** for motor faults, but only **0.01–0.32 for sensor-offset faults** that corrupt what a robot perceives without changing its gross motion ([Tarapore, Christensen & Timmis 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0182058)). O'Keeffe et al. close the gap by flagging **disagreement between the robot's self-estimate and its neighbours' exteroceptive estimate**. They diagnose 68–76 % of faults with 3–5 % false positives ([O'Keeffe et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7805982/)). The only VANET checks that catch a consistent liar are physical-layer ones. So, Petit and Starobinski's RSSI plausibility checks reach **95.91 % precision and 83.73 % recall** on modified VeReMi without assuming an honest majority ([So, Petit & Starobinski, WiSec 2019](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3317549.3323406)). LoRa reports RSSI and SNR per packet at no airtime cost, so the per-team-board future already has the needed channel. No published source evaluates it on LoRa, and on one board every "receiver" shares one geometry, so it cannot be tested honestly until the hardware split.

Some improvements are available now. VeReMi's history-consistency checks catch broken-GPS and random-offset bugs, though not a consistent liar. The TRON equivalents are grid rules: a step of 0 or 1 cell per tick, adjacency between successive positions, and monotone progress along the declared leg. They need no new radio work. The literature also offers a home for their results. RFSN keeps a **beta(α, β) reputation** per neighbour from binary watchdog outcomes, with trust as the beta mean ([Ganeriwal, Balzano & Srivastava 2008](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1362542.1362546)). A per-target beta counter of plausibility passes and fails would give a second axis, "do I believe t's reports?", alongside "is t stalled?". It should stay first-hand. VeReMi warns of "data-driven bad-mouthing" once opinions are shared. On the statistics, a structural zero baseline means **one stall is conclusive** (its likelihood ratio is infinite). The 2-of-4 watchdog rule is therefore a robustness rule, and at *q* = 0.2 it costs 4.5 ticks mean and 10 ticks at the 95th percentile, against 1.4 and 5 for a 1-stall rule (computed). CLEAR on 4 clean samples bounds the stall rate only to ≤ 0.527 at 95 % ([Hanley & Lippman-Hand 1983](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/385438); exact bound computed). The design keeps 2-of-4 for watchdog equivalence, which is defensible, but it should log healthy stalls. A single legitimate one breaks the zero-baseline assumption that makes the analysis work. Finally, the searches found **no published multi-robot search-and-rescue system doing peer fault voting over LoRa**. TRON appears to occupy a genuine gap, so there is no field benchmark to borrow.

## Conclusion

The literature points to a clear split. Per-peer detection is close to its information-theoretic limit, so tuning windows, swapping in CUSUM or adopting phi-accrual buys little. Fusion is where both the risks and the gains are. The risks are that turnout-dependent thresholds let silence and a couple of liars elevate a healthy target, and that the independence assumption hides correlated-loss failures. The gain is that sharing per-tick observations instead of verdicts turns a roughly 7-tick detection into a roughly 2-tick one, which is the only credible answer to R3 found in the literature. The design's claim that votes are independent of the target's *conclusions* holds up. Independence of *evidence* is the stricter standard WSN, swarm and VANET work applies, and TRON meets it only for communication. Mobility consensus stays redundancy, not corroboration, until a peer can measure something about the target that the target did not say.

Two things remain uncertain. None of the numbers here have been checked against real LoRa loss correlation, and the literature offers no calibration for how correlated loss is across receivers. The simulator's deterministic periodic faults also flatter every detector: under a randomized emission pattern, 4-of-6 catches only 71–90 % of fault windows (computed). Both belong in the test plan before any threshold is treated as final.
